The impurities that matter

You're buying the steel for the next batch of brackets. Three mills have quoted. The cheapest would make your scrapped brackets more than three times too radioactive; the most expensive one is overkill. The difference is a few hundredths of a percent of one element.

The order is 2 tonnes of SS316 plate. The steel's recipe fixes its main ingredients: iron, 17% chromium, 12% nickel and so on. It doesn't fix the trace elements each mill's scrap and ore bring along, so every batch comes with a certificate that lists what was measured.

Your site has one rule for parts like this, made up for this course but typical of real ones: ten years after the machine stops, a scrapped bracket must read under at its surface, so it can be handled and packed without remote tools. How can the page answer instantly for any certificate?

A trace element is so dilute that its atoms are activated independently of each other: twice the cobalt, twice the cobalt-60. So ACTINV runs the steel once without impurities and once per impurity, and the dose rate for any certificate is the steel's own plus each element's amount times its effect. actinv impurities checks this by rerunning the nominal steel in full and comparing; here they agreed to 2 parts in 10¹⁵.

Write the specification

The fix isn't to buy the purest steel. It's to ask for exactly what matters. A purchase specification sets a maximum for any element you name; anything you don't name can be anywhere in its usual range. Each line you add costs the mill testing and narrows who can bid, so use as few as you can.

Screen your own steel

Every number in this chapter came from two screens of the bracket steel, which you can download and run. The first lists each impurity's effect; the second, limit.json, adds the rule and asks for the largest amount of each impurity that keeps it.

In the desktop app:

  1. Download base.json and photon-response.json into one folder, and open base.json with Open problem….
  2. Open Impurities, under STUDIES. Keep Fe as the Balance.
  3. Add each trace element by its symbol, with the lowest, usual and highest amount you expect. Your own mills' certificates are the best source.
  4. Under What matters, pick the dose rate at the surface, set a limit, and pick the cooling time your rule applies at.
  5. Screen impurities. Each element gets its effect, the nuclides it acts through, and its largest allowed amount.

The command line does the same in one command, actinv impurities limit.json limit-result.json, and the limit is in the result:

Co  limit  0.0339
Cu  limit  43.7
Ag, B, N, Nb, Ta  no response

Copper has a limit too, but at 43.7% it is no limit at all. The other five can't reach the rule however much there is, before the iron runs out.

Planning maintenance

The machine stopped on 1 March. Three jobs are waiting in the port cell, and the parts are still radioactive. Go in too early and the crew can't legally work; wait too long and the outage costs more every day. Pick the day.

The work order has three jobs, done one after another by the same team on the same day:

The site's radiation rules, made up for this course but typical of real ones:

≤ 3 mSv/hNobody works where the dose rate is above this. It's the access limit. ≤ 20 mSvNobody receives more than this over the whole outage.

Both parts sat two years in chapter 1's port cell: the bracket, 20 kg of steel with 0.1% cobalt, and a 5 kg copper cooling pipe. ACTINV computed each one's dose rate at one metre, from a minute to a hundred years after shutdown. The pipe is under the access limit from the start; the bracket takes months to get there.

What a day is worth

The first allowed day isn't always the best one. Every day the machine stays off costs money (here a day), and every person-sievert of dose the crew takes has a cost too: an organisation's figure for what it's worth paying to avoid. Waiting longer means less dose and more outage. Pick the figure, and the planner shades each allowed day by its total cost.

Set the value to the highest figure. Where does the cheapest day move?
Pick, on the planner, a day within 1% of the cheapest plan's cost.

What waste is it?

A 50 kg support block of the course steel spent five years much closer to the plasma than chapter 1's bracket. Now it's coming out for good, and before anyone can ship it for burial, someone has to fill in the waste classification. Today that's you.

In the United States, 10 CFR 61.55 sorts waste for burial near the surface into classes A, B and C, from the concentration of certain nuclides in curies per cubic metre. Two tables decide: table 1 lists long-lived nuclides, table 2 short-lived ones. Each nuclide's concentration is divided by its limit and the fractions are added: the sum of fractions. A sum above 1 means the limit is broken.

ACTINV has printed the concentrations. The pencilled boxes are yours.

The certificate's worst case

The concentrations above are for the typical steel: 0.01% niobium and 0.05% nitrogen. The mill's certificate allows up to 0.05% niobium and 0.1% nitrogen. Niobium-93 captures a neutron and becomes niobium-94, which lasts 20,000 years; nitrogen-14 becomes carbon-14. A trace of either goes straight into table 1.

Let ACTINV fill it in

Every printed number on the worksheet came from one file, waste-screen.json: the steel's composition with each trace element's range, the cooling times, and the two classes to test. actinv impurities runs the block once per element, derives each nuclide's half-life from the decay data, and reports the class for the typical steel and for the worst the ranges allow.

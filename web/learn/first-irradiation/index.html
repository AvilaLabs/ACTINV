Step 2

What neutrons do to steel

A fusion machine fuses deuterium and tritium, and each fusion releases a fast neutron. Neutrons have no charge, so they fly straight out of the plasma and into everything nearby, including our steel bracket.

Usually a neutron passes straight through. Now and then it hits the nucleus of an atom and changes it. An iron atom might become a manganese atom, or a nickel atom might become a cobalt atom. This is called activation, and many of the new nuclides are unstable: sooner or later each one decays, giving off radiation. That is what makes the bracket radioactive.

Inside the bracket

This is a cartoon, not a simulation: real steel has about 10²⁴ atoms per kilogram, and only a tiny fraction ever change. Grey dots are stable atoms, coloured dots are radioactive ones, and each flash is a decay.

Half-life: how fast it builds and fades

Every radioactive nuclide has a half-life: the time for half of its atoms to decay. Half-lives run from fractions of a second to millions of years, and that one number decides most of what happens to a part after shutdown.

Under steady irradiation, a nuclide builds up until it decays as fast as it is made. It gets close to that ceiling after a few half-lives, and after the machine stops, its activity halves once every half-life. Try three nuclides that really are made in steel:

The formula, if you want it

For a nuclide made at a steady rate R with decay constant λ = ln 2 / half-life, the activity after irradiating for time t is A = R(1 − e−λt). After shutdown it falls as e−λt. ACTINV solves the same kind of equations, for thousands of nuclides at once, including chains where one radioactive nuclide decays into another.

Meet the bracket

Here is the job. A 20 kg bracket made of SS316 stainless steel sits in a port cell of a fusion machine for two years, where about 300 million neutrons cross each square centimetre of it every second (the flux). Then the machine shuts down for maintenance, and a crew needs to go in and unbolt the bracket.

ACTINV takes the whole question as one plain text file, a problem file. It is written in JSON, a format that both people and programs can read. Click each part of the file to see what it says.

            New to JSON? A 30-second tour
            
JSON is labels and values. "mass_g": 20000.0 means "the mass in grams is 20,000". Text goes in quotes, numbers don't, curly braces { } group related labels together, and square brackets [ ] hold a list. That's nearly all of it.

            
You rarely type a problem file from scratch. actinv new writes a complete one for you to edit, the app writes one as you click through its pages, and a script can write one.

          

        

        

          
◆ How ACTINV is different

          
The whole problem is one readable file. No fixed input-deck syntax, no set of files that must sit together under particular names. You can email it to a colleague, keep it under version control next to your design, or have a script write a hundred variations of it. Results come back as JSON too, so any tool, spreadsheet or plotting library can read them without parsing printed tables.

◆ How ACTINV is different

          
One engine, three ways in, and they don't drift apart. A file you build in the app runs unchanged from the command line or Python, and vice versa. The command line follows ordinary conventions: results can go straight into another program, errors say what to fix, and the exit code tells a script whether it worked. That's what lets ACTINV sit inside a larger workflow, such as a transport code's output feeding straight into it, instead of being a separate step you do by hand.

              Gray, sievert, and the fine print on this dose
              
ACTINV reports this dose as the rate of energy the photons would deposit in air (the air kerma), in grays per hour, with the bracket treated as a point one metre away. For the photon energies here, one gray of air kerma corresponds to roughly one sievert of dose to a person, so the page shows Sv/h. It's a screening number, good for "how long should we wait?". A real dose map of a port cell needs a radiation transport calculation, and ACTINV can feed one.

            

          

        

      


      

        
Step 7

        
The cobalt twist

        
SS316's standard recipe says nothing about cobalt, so ordinary steel usually contains a little: 0.05 to 0.2% is common, and nuclear-grade steel is bought with a limit on it. That's an impurity. A neutron turns cobalt-59 into cobalt-60, which has a 5.3-year half-life and gives off energetic gamma rays. Cobalt-60 also forms from nickel, which this steel has plenty of, but much more slowly.

        
Guess again: if this bracket had 0.1% cobalt, when would it matter most?

Short-lived nuclides, such as manganese-56 and chromium-51, come from the main ingredients and dominate the first days. They don't care about a trace of cobalt. Years later they're gone, and what's left is set by the impurities. For waste disposal and long-term maintenance, the impurity list in the purchase order matters as much as the alloy's name.

          

            
◆ How ACTINV is different

            
It tells you which impurities matter before you buy the steel. Here we checked one impurity by hand. actinv impurities checks every element at once: in one command it lists which trace elements drive the dose, heat or waste class of your part, and how much of each you can tolerate, ready for a purchase specification.

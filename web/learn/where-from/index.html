Where does it come from?

The bracket from chapter 1 is radioactive, but steel isn't made of cobalt-60 or manganese-54. Each radioactive nuclide was made from an ordinary atom. This chapter traces them back, and shows why that tells you what you can and can't change.

In chapter 1 you found that cobalt-60 showed up even in steel with no cobalt in it, and that a little cobalt made the bracket much harder to handle years later. Both facts come from the same question: which atom turned into which nuclide, and by what reaction? ACTINV calls the answer a route.

This chapter uses the same bracket: 20 kg of SS316 with 0.1% cobalt, two years in chapter 1's neutron field, looked at one day after shutdown. All numbers come from ACTINV runs of the problem file you can download.

Step 2 of 7

Four reactions make most of it

When a neutron hits a nucleus, a handful of things can happen. Four of them make nearly everything radioactive in steel. They're written as (n,x): a neutron goes in, x comes out.

  • (n,γ), "n-gamma": the nucleus captures the neutron and gets one mass unit heavier. Same element. Slow neutrons are best at it.
  • (n,p): the neutron goes in and a proton comes out, so the element changes to the one below it. Needs a fast neutron.
  • (n,2n): a very fast neutron knocks a second one out. Same element, one mass unit lighter.
  • (n,α): an alpha particle (two protons, two neutrons) comes out. Two elements down. Needs a fast neutron.

Switch to "Only 14 MeV neutrons" and look at cobalt-60 again. Where did its biggest route go?
Why is cobalt-60 split into two routes through Co-60m?

Capture on cobalt-59 often leaves cobalt-60 in an excited, isomeric state, Co-60m, which settles to ordinary cobalt-60 in about ten minutes. ACTINV keeps the two states apart, so a route can pass through either. Both end in the same nuclide, so for this question you can add them up.

Why does it say a route is "integrated exactly"?

          
Some codes estimate a route's share from a simplified chain formula. ACTINV solves each route as its own small chain, with every nuclide's full loss rate and the same irradiation history as the main calculation, so the shares of a nuclide's routes add up to the atoms ACTINV actually computed. The result says how much is explained: explained_fraction is 0.999 or more for every nuclide here.

          What ACTINV does differently
          
Routes on request, in the same run. One extra block in the problem file gives every route to the nuclides you name, with exact shares and how much of each nuclide the routes explain. It works for any material and history ACTINV can run, and the answer comes back in the same JSON result as everything else, ready for a script, a notebook or the app's Pathways page.

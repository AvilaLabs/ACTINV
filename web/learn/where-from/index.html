Where does it come from?

Steel isn't made of cobalt-60 or manganese-54, yet one day after shutdown the bracket from chapter 1 is full of them. Every radioactive atom in it used to be an ordinary one. Find out which.

Here's the bracket one day after the machine stopped: 20 kg of SS316 steel with 0.1% cobalt, after two years in chapter 1's neutron field. The bar is its dose rate one metre away, , split up by the nuclide that gives it off.

0

Each piece is a case to solve: which stable atom in the steel turned into this nuclide? Solved cases turn blue.

Each nuclide was made by a nuclear reaction, from an atom that was already in the steel. ACTINV calls each chain from a starting atom to a nuclide a route, and can list every route with its share. Before you see ACTINV's answer, you'll work it out from the chart of nuclides. You need four moves for that.

How atoms move

This is a piece of the chart of nuclides, the map nuclear engineers use. Each row is an element, from nickel at the top to chromium at the bottom. Each column is a number of neutrons. The grey squares are the stable atoms in steel, with how common each one is in its element.

A neutron reaction moves an atom from one square to another, always by the same step. Pick a grey square, then a move:

Why do some moves need fast neutrons?

Capture, (n,γ), only adds a neutron, and slow neutrons are captured best: they spend longer near each nucleus. The other three knock something out of the nucleus, a proton, a second neutron or an alpha particle, and that takes energy. Only a neutron that arrives with several million electronvolts can pay for it. The 14 MeV neutrons straight from a fusion plasma can.

    Some routes go through "Co-60m". What's that?

    Capture on cobalt-59 often leaves cobalt-60 in an excited, isomeric state, Co-60m, which settles to ordinary cobalt-60 in about ten minutes. ACTINV keeps the two states apart and reports the routes through each. Both end in the same square, so the chart adds them up.

    Same steel, other places

    The routes depend on where the steel sits. Chapter 1's bracket is in a port cell, where many neutrons have been slowed down by everything around them. Put the same steel against the wall that faces the plasma and nearly every neutron arrives at 14 MeV. Or buy steel with no cobalt in it. Pick a nuclide and a place, and watch the routes change.

    Look at cobalt-60 facing the plasma. Where did the blue arrow go?

    The plasma-facing run has the same total flux as the port cell, all of it at 14 MeV. That is four times as many fast neutrons, which is why the fast routes grow.
